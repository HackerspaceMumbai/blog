# Hacktoberfest Hack Day Mumbai 2026: Building in Public, One Checkpoint at a Time

Most technology events are measured by the number of talks delivered.

Hacktoberfest Hack Day Mumbai was measured by progress.

Every thirty minutes, participants shared what they had built, what they had learned, and what they planned to tackle next.

By the end of the day, those updates had evolved into 18 project submissions, hundreds of community conversations, recruitment opportunities, and a room full of builders that simply refused to stop hacking.

On 17 October 2026, builders from across Mumbai gathered for **Hacktoberfest Hack Day Mumbai**, a community-driven Open Source and AI buildathon organized by **Hackerspace Mumbai** and hosted by our ecosystem partner **Paytm** at its Waterstones Corporate Park office.

This was not a conference.

There were no keynote sessions.

No panel discussions.

No sponsor presentations consuming half the agenda.

We kept the introduction short, explained the rules, distributed the challenges, and got out of the way.

The day belonged to the builders.

## From 500+ Registrations to a Room Full of Builders

Long before Hack Day began, it became clear that community interest had exceeded our expectations.

More than **500 registrations** poured in alongside a steady stream of questions, emails, comments, messages, and requests for clarification.

- Who would be approved?
- Could participants work solo?
- How did project submissions work?
- What was the relationship between Luma approvals and MLH registration?
- How would judging be conducted?
- What qualified for swag?

The challenge was not simply running the event.

The challenge was curating it.

Hack Day was designed as a focused, builder-first experience, so applications were carefully reviewed to ensure a balance of first-time contributors, experienced developers, students, maintainers, and AI enthusiasts.

That effort paid off.

When the day finally arrived, the room was not filled with spectators.

It was filled with builders.

## Building in Public

One challenge with any hackathon is ensuring that projects are genuinely built during the event itself.

We wanted participants to share their journey, not just their final demo.

To make that happen, we introduced progress checkpoints every 30 minutes.

Throughout the afternoon, builders shared updates about:

- What they were building
- What they had completed
- What challenges they were facing
- What they planned to work on next

Those updates were posted publicly through our event channels and community platforms, creating a visible and verifiable timeline of every project's evolution.

The checkpoint system proved to be one of the day's most successful experiments.

It encouraged accountability.

It encouraged collaboration.

It helped participants discover others building similar things.

Most importantly, it provided a transparent record showing how ideas transformed into working software.

## Everyone Was Building, Including the Organizer 😅

A funny thing happened while preparing for Hack Day.

After reviewing more than **500 registrations** and answering the same questions across Luma, LinkedIn, email, social media, and community channels, I realized I was spending nearly as much time answering attendee questions as organizing the event itself.

The obvious solution?

Build the thing every organizer dreams about after answering the same question for the fiftieth time.

Inspired by Hacktoberfest's AI theme, I started building **HackBuddy**, an open-source AI event concierge designed to answer common attendee questions and reduce organizer overhead.

Of course, in true hackathon fashion, I only managed to start coding it **during Hack Day itself**, which meant it was completely unavailable for the event it was originally supposed to help with.

So while participants were building hackathon projects, I was quietly building one of my own.

# 🏆 Overall Winner: Kavach

Selecting winners proved surprisingly difficult.

Projects were evaluated on real-world impact, technical execution, innovation, effective use of Open Source AI, and the ability to demonstrate meaningful progress throughout the day.

Among the 18 submissions, one project consistently stood out.

Built by **Kratik Jain**, **Kavach** is an open-source offline scam-call guard designed to help identify potentially fraudulent calls using local AI.

Rather than relying on cloud processing, Kavach analyzes conversations locally, helping users understand potential risks while maintaining privacy.

### A Snapshot of Where Open Source AI Is Heading

Looking across all 18 submissions, several themes emerged:

## When Hack Day Created Opportunity

One of the most interesting outcomes of the day was not listed anywhere in the schedule.

Throughout the event, members of the Paytm engineering and leadership teams spent time interacting with participants, reviewing projects, and observing teams as they worked.

Unlike traditional recruitment processes, they were not evaluating résumés.

They were watching people build.
<Image 
  src={PaytmEngagement}
  alt="Paytm engineering team engaging with participants"
  width={1200}
/>

They saw how participants approached challenges, worked through technical issues, collaborated with teammates, adapted to feedback, and communicated ideas.

As Paytm engineering leadership noted, hackathons are often one of the best ways to discover talent because they reveal how people actually work.

As a result, recruitment opportunities were extended to several participants, and multiple builders progressed into interview conversations.

The recruitment process remains ongoing.

Sometimes the strongest résumé is not a PDF.

It is a working project.

## The Schedule Ended. The Building Did Not.

The original schedule was straightforward.

- 2:30 PM — Hacking begins
- 4:30 PM — Submissions close
- 4:45 PM — Judging begins
- 5:15 PM — Judging ends
- 5:30 PM — Closing activities

But something interesting happened.

People kept building.

Submissions kept improving.

Judges kept asking questions.

Builders kept refining demos.

Conversations kept going.

What was originally expected to conclude around 5:30 PM stretched deep into the evening.

Final judging discussions wrapped up around **8:30 PM**.

Nearly three additional hours.

Frankly, nobody seemed in a hurry to leave.

That is probably the strongest endorsement of the event we could have hoped for.
